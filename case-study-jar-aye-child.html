Making Hard Math
Feel Tractable.

How do you make rigorous HOTS math feel approachable to a 7-year-old -- without dumbing it down? The app has 3 seconds before the child taps away.

01 -- Three Seconds to Lose Her

Maya opens a combinatorics question. Reads it twice. Doesn't know where to start.

The app has three seconds before she taps away. Not because the content is boring -- but because the interface offers no foothold. She sees a wall of text and a blank answer field. No scaffold. No hint of where to begin. The cognitive load hits before the learning can start.

The design challenge is precise: make rigorous mathematical reasoning feel tractable without diluting its cognitive demands. This is not a gamification problem. It is a scaffolding problem.

The Constraint

One product. Three users with competing anxieties. The child fears being wrong. The parent fears wasting money. The teacher fears missing who needs help. Every screen must serve all three without compromising any.

Before designing anything, we needed to understand what real child behavior actually looks like.
02 -- Three Jobs to Be Done

Three users. Three anxieties. One product.

The Child -- Feel capable and make progress

Anxiety: getting something wrong, not knowing what to do next, looking stupid in front of peers. Every state must answer: does this make a child feel safe enough to try again?

The Parent -- Know if the money is working

Anxiety: can't tell if their child genuinely understands or is just clicking buttons to earn points.

The Teacher -- Identify who needs help before the test

Anxiety: manually analyzing 30 individual student accounts to find the three who are falling behind.

It's a subscription product. Over-optimizing for engagement risks teaching children to chase points rather than understand mathematics. The design must reward persistence and understanding -- not just correct answers.

What does real child behavior actually look like when they encounter difficulty?
03 -- Observed, Not Self-Reported

Five children. Competitor products. Unmediated behavior.

We ran remote sessions in Mandalay with five children using competing Singaporean edtech products -- not Jar Aye. This removed product-specific bias entirely. We watched navigation patterns, confusion responses, and difficulty-handling in real sessions, not inferred from what children say they'd do.

Why this approach: assumptions about how children learn are easy to get wrong. We watched real children use real products before designing anything.

  • Finding 1: 100% navigated core flows successfully -- validating that a similar navigation structure would minimize the learning curve.
  • Finding 2: One child went silent on confusion. Didn't ask for help. Didn't retry. Just stopped. This informed the need for built-in question explanations that appear automatically.
Research Principle

Children don't self-report. Observational research produces different -- and more honest -- data than interviews. We designed for what we saw, not what children told us.

04 -- Sign-In: Remove the Failure Mode

Error prevention over error recovery.

Primary 1 children often don't know their email address. A traditional email-and-password login is a failure mode before the product even begins. Every design decision on this screen is about preventing the error from being possible in the first place.

  • Username, not email -- School-assigned username removes the failure mode entirely. The child never needs to type an address they don't know.
  • Segmented 4-box PIN -- Chunking into four visible cells reduces working memory load. The child always knows how many characters remain.
  • Disabled until both fields filled -- Error prevention. The child never sees a "wrong" state from an empty submission.
With the child logged in, the next question is: what does the learning loop look like?
05 -- The Learning Loop

Video before question. Self-check before report.

The learning loop follows a deliberate sequence: Class, Topic, Video, Question, Feedback, Self Check, Report. Each step exists for a pedagogical reason. Video comes before the question because novice learners benefit from seeing a solved problem before attempting their own. The self-check asks the learner to evaluate their own understanding -- a metacognitive step most edtech products skip entirely.

What happens when the child actually faces a question and gets it wrong?
06 -- Five Question States

Each state is deliberate. Each state teaches.

  • Default / Filled -- Clean state. No noise until ready to submit. Disabled submit prevents empty submissions.
  • Correct -- Celebration header appears but the question and illustration remain. Keeps the cognitive link between the right answer and the reasoning.
  • Wrong -- Solution pop-up shows the reasoning, not just the answer. The child compares the solution to their own thinking.
  • Two CTAs after correct -- "Show Solution" (secondary) and "Next Question" (primary). Serves both the intuitive guesser and the child who understood the process.
  • Fixed state -- After viewing the solution, the corrected answer state. Builds the mental model for next time.
The question states handle individual problems. But how do you teach a child how to think, not just what to answer?
07 -- HOTS Framework as Interface

Pedagogy as navigable screen states, not a teacher guide PDF.

Higher-Order Thinking Skills are typically locked inside teacher training documents. We translated five pedagogical strategies into interactive screen states that children navigate directly. Each strategy is a distinct UI pattern, not a hidden algorithm.

  • Making a Guess -- Activates prior knowledge. The child commits to an estimate before reasoning -- reducing blank-page paralysis.
  • Using a Representation -- Draw it. Table it. Diagram it. Externalizes working memory so the child can see their thinking.
  • Walking Through the Process -- Step-by-step reasoning scaffold. Makes thinking explicit and auditable.
  • Changing the Problem -- What if the numbers were different? Builds transfer to novel problems.
  • Adventure Quest Challenge -- Extension gated behind lesson completion. A genuine leaderboard differentiator that rewards depth.
How do you make progress legible to both a seven-year-old and their parent?
08 -- 4-Tier Mastery System

Progress that a child can feel and a parent can verify.

TierRangeDesign Rationale
Mastery80-100%Clear achievement signal for both child and parent.
Competent60-79%Positive framing -- capable, still growing.
Beginning0-59%Non-judgmental. A starting point, not a failing grade.
Incomplete<10 attemptsSeparates "hasn't done it yet" from "attempted and failed."

"24 out of 120 skills" provides a concrete, countable north-star metric. More meaningful than a percentage dial. Each wrong and correct answer is color-coded and dated in the attempt history -- for parents, diagnostic data; for children, a record of improvement.

Progress tracking handles the cognitive side. But what about the emotional side -- engagement without anxiety?
09 -- Engagement Without Anxiety

Forgiving mechanics. No streak punishment.

A Duolingo-style streak counter punishes missed days. For children whose screen time is parent-controlled, that punishment is both unfair and anxiety-producing. We replaced streaks with a calendar grid that accumulates without punishment.

  • Calendar, not streak counter -- Accumulates without punishment. Appropriate for children whose screen time is parent-controlled.
  • Achievement sharing -- Parent-targeted, not child-to-child. Sharing to Instagram and Telegram drives organic acquisition without peer comparison.
  • Grade-filtered leaderboard -- A Primary 1 student competes with Primary 1 students only. Psychologically safe comparison.
  • Jar Yit Points for completion -- Points earned for lesson completion, not correct answers alone. A struggling child who finishes the lesson still earns social standing. Rewards persistence.
The engagement model keeps children coming back. But what happens when the subscription expires?
10 -- Subscription States

Expired, frozen, and trial -- each is a semantic contract.

Subscription states are not just technical conditions. They are promises to the user. Each label carries emotional weight and must be chosen carefully.

Trial Expired

Content remains browsable but gated with a clear upgrade path. Show what the child is missing -- not a blank wall.

Subscription Expired

Expired tags on lesson cards with a visible renewal path. The child sees what they had and could have again.

Subscription Frozen

State preserved. No progress lost. "Frozen" implies temporary -- prevents parents from assuming they've lost their child's history.

Every design decision has trade-offs. Here is what I would revisit.
11 -- What I Would Revisit

Three decisions I'd reconsider with more data.

Navigation Discoverability

Hamburger menu was a deliberate trade-off for screen real estate. For young users, a bottom nav bar would be more persistent and legible. Worth testing.

Video-First Assumption

Lower-proficiency students may need a simplified example problem, not just a passive video. Active scaffolding might outperform passive demonstration for struggling learners.

Parent Visibility Gap

The report gives excellent data after a problem develops. A push notification on consistent failure would close this loop proactively rather than reactively.

Despite these open questions, the results told a clear story.
