01 -- The Doubt

The prototype looks right. But does it work?

It's 11pm. The client demo is at 9am. Marcus has been staring at his onboarding flow for three weeks. He can recite every screen transition from memory. That familiarity is the problem -- he can no longer tell whether the flow is genuinely intuitive or whether he has simply memorized every path through it.

He sends the Figma link to four colleagues. Three replies trickle in over three hours: one says it looks clean, another says it felt busy, and the third sends a thumbs-up emoji. None of them told him which screen caused confusion. None of them followed the intended path. None of them gave him evidence.

"I've been staring at this onboarding flow for three weeks. I can't tell if it's genuinely intuitive or if I've just memorized every path through it."

-- Marcus, Product Designer (Composite persona from 8 designer interviews)
So what are designers actually doing when they're under pressure and the demo is tomorrow?
02 -- What 8 Designers Told Us

Semi-structured interviews. 30 minutes each.

I spoke with 8 product designers about their testing habits and pain points. The conversations were structured around three themes: when they test, what feedback they receive, and what would change their behavior. The pattern was remarkably consistent.

  • 6 out of 8 skip testing under deadline pressure. Testing is the first thing cut when the timeline compresses.
  • 7 out of 8 receive vague, unusable feedback. Colleagues give vibes, not evidence.
  • 8 out of 8 said they would test more if it took under five minutes. Willingness exists. Infrastructure does not.

Three blockers emerged from these conversations. First, coordination tax -- scheduling testers takes half a day of logistics for ninety minutes of actual data. Second, unstructured feedback -- the responses designers receive lack diagnostic value. Third, schedule mismatch -- the moment you most urgently need to validate never aligns with anyone else's calendar.

Core Design Target

This finding set the north star: time to first structured feedback must be under 60 seconds. Not faster testing -- instant testing.

03 -- The Core Bet

Simulate, don't recruit.

If AI can simulate how different user types approach a UI -- their decision patterns, hesitations, and assumptions -- designers get structured usability feedback at any hour, on any prototype, with zero coordination overhead.

The bet was specific: use AI-driven personas navigating a live prototype via Puppeteer to produce step-by-step behavioral evidence. Not heuristic checklists. Not design linting. Actual simulated navigation with observable reasoning.

ApproachVerdictWhy
Heuristic ChecklistRejectedMeasures against rules. No path evidence.
Figma API AutomationRejectedBrittle. Breaks when component names change. Tool-locked.
AI + PuppeteerChosenScreenshot-based detection works for any prototype URL. Persona-driven prompts produce behavior.

The non-obvious call: Puppeteer over Figma API. We traded tight integration for tool-agnostic flexibility. Any prototype URL works -- Figma, InVision, hosted staging environments.

Five stages. One purpose: evidence before the demo.

The flow was designed to get a designer from zero to structured feedback in under three minutes. Each stage collapses what previously took hours of coordination into a single focused action.

05 -- Plan the Test

Define what success looks like before the AI touches the prototype.

The test setup screen asks four questions. A user goal in plain language -- what the persona is trying to accomplish. A maximum step count to cap AI navigation. The metrics to track: steps, backtracks, pass or fail. And the personas to run: PM, Developer, Designer -- each with distinct behavioral traits injected into their system prompt.

With the test defined, the designer needs to show the AI what the goal state actually looks like.
06 -- Set the Golden Path

Navigate to the goal. Click once. AI knows what success is.

The designer navigates through a live Puppeteer session of their prototype. When they reach the intended goal state, they click "Set as Goal Screen." The AI receives both the text goal and a screenshot as a dual-signal target -- making detection robust even when dynamic content shifts pixels around.

Now the AI has a clear destination. Time to watch it navigate.
07 -- See the Evidence

Step-by-step reasoning. Not just a pass/fail.

The results page shows what the AI did at every decision point and why. Not just which button it clicked -- but what it observed on screen, what it considered, and what it dismissed. Zero backtracks means the path was confident. Three or more backtracks signals confusion worth investigating.

A sample result: Pass. Five steps. Zero backtracks. The AI clicked "Get Started" because the primary CTA was visible and prominent. It skipped the tooltip because the tooltip was not blocking. It reached the goal screen through the intended path.

But does AI actually match real user behavior? That's the question that determines whether this tool is useful or just impressive.
08 -- Validation

AI vs. Real Users: 9 out of 11 blockers matched.

We tested three prototype flows with three designers each, comparing AI navigation paths against human navigation paths. The results: 9 out of 11 navigation blockers identified by AI matched real user behavior. Where AI reported backtracks, real users also hesitated or took wrong paths.

What AI cannot do -- by design -- is judge aesthetic preference. Visual polish, brand tone, emotional resonance: these remain human territory. Golden Paths de-risks interaction flows between formal research rounds. It does not replace research.

09 -- Key Design Decisions

Four deliberate trade-offs.

Free-Form Goal Text

Natural language over dropdowns. "Reach the dashboard without backtracking" is more honest than any preset option. It forces the designer to articulate their actual success criteria.

Screenshot-Based Detection

Works regardless of prototype tool. Figma, InVision, hosted staging -- all handled identically. No integration dependencies to maintain or break.

Linear Stage Machine

No back navigation mid-session. Focus over flexibility. Eliminating choice paralysis in the setup flow keeps the designer moving forward.

Steps, Not Time

Step count is more diagnostic than seconds elapsed. A user who takes 30 seconds on the right path is fine. A user who takes 10 seconds but backtracks twice is not.

11 -- Resolution

Marcus's story, resolved.

He opened Golden Paths. Defined the success state in three minutes. Ran an AI test with a PM persona -- forty-seven seconds. One below-fold button was flagged as a navigation obstacle. He fixed it in four minutes. Re-ran the test. Clean navigation. No backtracks.

He went to sleep knowing the demo would land.

Impact

6 out of 8 designers shipped untested prototypes under deadline pressure before Golden Paths. 100% of early adopters ran validation on every major iteration after.

12 -- What Broke First

Three failures that shaped the final product.

AI Hallucinated Screens

Early prompt: "navigate to the goal." The AI invented click targets that didn't exist on screen. Fix: constrain navigation to visible elements only.

Personas Felt Identical

All three personas clicked the same way. Same paths, same decisions. Fix: inject behavioral traits directly into each persona's system prompt -- not just role labels.

Goal Detection Too Strict

Pixel-perfect screenshot matching failed on dynamic content -- loading spinners, timestamps, cursor positions. Fix: dual-signal with 80% match threshold instead of exact comparison.

Every failure made the product more robust. Here is what I took away.
13 -- What This Taught Me

Four lessons from building Golden Paths.

Eliminate Logistics, Not Research

The tool de-risks prototypes so that human research sessions produce signal, not basic corrections. AI handles the first pass. Humans handle the depth.

Show, Don't Describe

Screenshot-based goal detection is more reliable than words alone. Dual-signal -- text plus visual -- beats single-signal every time.

Emergent Over Encoded

Prompt-based persona context produces more realistic behavior than hard-coded decision rules. Let the AI reason, don't script it.

Craft Multiplier

When testing costs sixty seconds instead of three hours, designers test every sprint -- not once a quarter. Lower the cost and the behavior changes.
