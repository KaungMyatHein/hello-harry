01 — The Problem

The red flag.

It started with a single sentence at a workshop in Maldives.

Cashiers are struggling with the new return process. They can't understand the design… but we don't know why.
Colleague · Maldives workshop · the sentence that started the project

That was all I needed to hear. I started watching cashiers work. Not through analytics dashboards — in person, on the shop floor, during real transactions.

What I saw was painful. Every hidden button, every missing label cost seconds. Not dramatic seconds — the kind nobody notices individually. But multiply a 30-second delay by thousands of transactions per day, across every store in the chain, and the cost becomes enormous.

The POS wasn't broken. It was invisibly expensive.

I needed to find exactly where the friction lived — and prove it.
02 — Audit

Four features, all invisible.

I audited the interface against the tasks cashiers performed most. Four features stood out — not because they were complex, but because they were hidden.

01 · Critical Return Process

Buried so deep that cashiers couldn't find it without asking a manager. The exact friction the workshop flagged.

02 · Daily Day End

Icon only, no text label. New hires had no chance of finding it without a buddy.

03 · High-frequency Discounts

Two clicks too many for a task done dozens of times daily. Hidden cost compounds at scale.

04 · Recurring Reprint

High frequency, low visibility. Customers waited while cashiers hunted through menus.

Four critical daily tasks. All invisible in the interface.

Which of these should we fix first? And how do we decide without guessing?
03 — Research

Same questions, two audiences.

I could have asked stakeholders what matters most and started designing. Instead, I ran the same importance-ranking exercise twice — once with business stakeholders, once with cashiers on the shop floor.

Identical scale, identical activity list. Two heat maps. Then I overlaid them.

The business map. Internal stakeholders voted on each activity's importance (1-5 scale). The result told me what leadership thought mattered.

The cashier map. Same questions, same format, asked of the people doing the work. The result told me what actually caused friction every shift.

The surprise

Total, Reprint, Void, and Hold scored high on both sides — clear mandate. The real surprise was Returns. Business rated them low — "returns are rare." Cashiers rated them as their #1 frustration.

Why? Returns weren't frequent. But every single return had a customer standing at the counter, watching, waiting. The per-incident impact was enormous — and invisible in aggregate data.

Now we had evidence-based priorities. Time to redesign — starting with the feature everyone underestimated.

Return scored highest — the exact feature that started this project, the one business had dismissed as "rare." The dual-audience overlay validated the approach: we were fixing the right thing.

But clarity scores only tell you if people can find things. The real question: would it actually save time at scale?
05 — Solution

What changed.

Four structural changes, each solving a specific pain point from the audit.

  • Grouped Activities — Related actions in one place. No more hunting across menus.
  • Sorted by Frequency — The interface reflects how cashiers work, not how engineers organized the database.
  • Icon + Text Labels — 48px touch targets. New hires no longer memorize icon meanings.
  • Product-Level Actions — One tap on the product, not a detour through a menu.
The prototype tested well. But could we put a number on the business impact?

07 — Reflection

What this project taught me.

Four lessons I take into every brief now. The most important: observation beats assumption — every single time.

Lesson 01 We almost redesigned the wrong thing

The first stakeholder conversation pointed us toward the sale flow. It was only when I watched cashiers directly — not through reports, not through surveys — that returns emerged as the real friction point. Observation beats assumption every time.

Lesson 02 Low frequency does not mean low impact

Returns were "rare" in aggregate. But every slow return had a customer standing at the counter, watching, waiting. The impact per incident was enormous — and completely invisible in dashboard metrics.

Lesson 03 Design debt compounds like financial debt

The assumption "returns are rare" wasn't wrong once. It was wrong for years — creating hidden costs that grew with every new store, every new cashier, every new deployment. Nobody questioned it because nobody measured it.

Lesson 04 Data wins stakeholder buy-in

The overlaid business-vs-cashier heat map made prioritization objective. When I showed it to leadership, the conversation shifted from "I think we should…" to "the data shows we should…" Stakeholders agreed because it was evidence, not opinion.

Time Machine

If I could go back.

One thing I'd absolutely redo: testing protocol. Lab conditions hid the friction we needed to see most.

What I assumed
Pre-launch usability testing
We planned for controlled lab conditions with structured tasks.
What I missed
Lab ≠ Real environment
Cashiers in a quiet room behave differently than cashiers during rush hour.
What I'd do
Shadow testing in stores
Run testing in actual retail locations during peak hours.
Expected outcome
Catch edge cases
Surface real-world friction that lab testing couldn't reveal.
