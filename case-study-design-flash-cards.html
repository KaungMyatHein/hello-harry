01 — Origin

Learning Design
Should Be Free

Myanmar has no structured design education pipeline. Junior designers learn by copying Dribbble without understanding the "why". I wanted a tool that teaches principles through testing — not tutorials.

The goal was simple: if you can't name a principle without prompting, you don't know it yet.

"Most design education is passive. You read, you nod, you forget."

02 — Problem

Passive Learning
Doesn't Stick

Reading Medium articles and watching YouTube tutorials creates an illusion of learning. The Ebbinghaus forgetting curve shows that without active retrieval practice, 80% of information is lost within a week.

The problem isn't access to information. It's the format.

03 — Design

One Card. Two Languages.
One Concept.

Each card has a front (English concept name) and back (English definition + Myanmar translation). Category filters let you drill specific domains. A progress ring shows session completion.

  • Monochrome cards — removes visual distraction, keeps focus on the content
  • Myanmar script in supporting role — native explanation, not a translation crutch
  • No timer — removes anxiety from the learning loop entirely
  • Keyboard shortcuts — for designers who prefer not to reach for the mouse

05 — Reflection

What I'd Do
Differently

01 — Content is the hard part.
Writing 100+ good card pairs took longer than building the entire UI. The quality of the prompt matters more than the design. I'd start with content architecture next time, not component architecture.
02 — Myanmar font rendering varies.
On some Android devices, the Myanmar script renders incorrectly. I'd choose a better web-safe Myanmar font from day one — and test on low-end Android hardware before launch, not after.
03 — Ship earlier.
I spent time on animations that nobody notices. The card flip itself was enough. The real value is in the content — and that only improves through user feedback, not more polish.
