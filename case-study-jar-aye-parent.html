01 -- The Invisible Investment

Aye pays. Her daughter uses the app. She has no way to tell if it's working.

Aye sees her daughter using the app every evening after school. The screen shows colorful math problems, progress bars, and point counters. But Aye cannot tell whether her daughter is learning -- or just clicking buttons to earn points. The content is Higher-Order Thinking Skills mathematics. Aye is not a math expert. She cannot evaluate her daughter's comprehension herself.

"I cannot directly observe whether the investment is working. The content is HOTS mathematics -- I can't evaluate her comprehension myself."

-- Aye, Parent of Two (Paying subscriber, not a math expert)

This creates a specific product design problem: the person paying for the subscription is not the person using the product. Every screen the parent sees must justify the subscription cost through data she can understand -- not mathematical concepts she cannot evaluate.

What constraints does this create for every screen in the parent app?
02 -- Five Constraints That Shaped Every Screen

The paying user is never the primary user.

  • Paying User != Primary User -- Aye's relationship with the product is entirely mediated through her child's data. Every screen must justify the subscription cost.
  • Domain is Technical -- HOTS math is not something most parents can assess. The app must translate raw performance into human-readable signals.
  • Multiple Children -- One parent account, multiple child profiles. The manage-child flow must support family-level oversight.
  • Emotionally Charged Subscription -- Renewal, freezing, and cancellation are high-stakes moments. These flows must feel trustworthy, not manipulative.
  • Time-Poor Parent -- The critical signal must land in seconds. Depth available on demand, but the first screen can never require more than a glance.
Design Principle

Every parent-facing screen must answer one question: "Is this worth it?"

Who informed these constraints? And how did we compensate for a small research sample?
03 -- The Client as Domain Expert

Primary source: a parent who lives the problem.

The client is a parent themselves -- a domain expert on the parent experience. Rather than external recruitment, the client served as the primary knowledge source for pain points, mental models, and emotional triggers around educational subscription products.

To compensate for single-source risk, we cross-referenced insights against published parent UX studies in edtech -- Duolingo family plan patterns, Khan Academy parent dashboard research, and SplashLearn engagement reports. The report screen was built with A/B test capability for post-launch validation.

Job 1: Know whether money and time is working

The report screen must make this immediately legible. No interpretation required.

Job 2: Manage subscription without confusion

Renewal, freeze, and cancel must feel transparent. One ambiguous label causes a support ticket.

Job 3: Feel pride in child's achievement

Shareable milestones that let parents celebrate publicly -- organic acquisition through pride.

04 -- Registration: Parent as Controller

Separation of concerns from the first screen.

The registration flow mirrors the child app login -- same username and PIN model, same visual language. This is deliberate. Consistency across both apps reduces cognitive overhead and reinforces product trust. The parent recognizes the system their child uses.

  • Username, not email -- Consistent with the child app. Reduces friction on mobile and aligns with family device management.
  • PIN over password -- Reinforces the unified product identity across both apps.
  • Forgot PIN flow -- Parents should never feel locked out of a product they're paying for.
  • Mirrors child app login -- Same mental model. Reduces cognitive overhead and reinforces product trust.
With the parent logged in, we arrive at the most strategic screen in the product.
05 -- The Report Screen

This is how the product justifies its subscription cost.

A parent who opens the report and immediately understands their child's status is a parent who renews. A parent who opens it confused is a parent who cancels. The report screen is not a dashboard. It is the product's renewal argument, rendered as data.

  • "24 out of 120 skills mastery" -- A number a parent can track over time. Concrete, countable, meaningful without mathematical expertise.
  • "Incomplete" is not zero -- Separates "hasn't attempted" from "attempted and failed." Prevents a gap from reading as a failing grade.
  • Attempt history with timestamps -- Each attempt is color-coded and dated. Parents see the process of improvement, not just a final score.

The report translates raw HOTS math performance into language a non-expert parent can read in under 50 seconds. No mathematical knowledge required. No interpretation ambiguity.

The report handles comprehension. But what about pride and subscription trust?
06 -- Social Pride & Subscription Trust

Pride drives organic acquisition. Trust prevents churn.

Hall of Fame

Achievement sharing to Instagram and Telegram is parent-targeted. A parent sharing their child's badge is organic acquisition -- low-cost referral without peer comparison pressure on the child.

Freeze vs Cancel

"Frozen" means temporary and reversible. "Cancelled" means permanent. Separate flows with distinct labels prevent accidental loss of progress history. The semantic difference matters.

Manage Subscription

Active plan details, renewal dates, and upgrade paths visible upfront. Reduces the "what am I paying for?" question that precedes cancellation.

Locked Content as Visible Aspiration

Expired tags on lesson cards show what the child is missing. Drives renewal more effectively than a blocking modal. Show the value, don't hide it.

Every design has trade-offs. Here is what I would revisit with more data.
07 -- What I Would Revisit

Four priorities for the next iteration.

Priority 1: Cross-Child Dashboard

For parents managing multiple children, no family-level view exists. A family summary would reduce the navigation overhead of switching between profiles.

Priority 2: Proactive Reporting Alerts

Weekly digest email summarizing the child's week. Closes the engagement loop between sessions without requiring the parent to open the app.

Priority 3: Subscription Transparency

Next billing date and amount should be visible on the home screen, not buried in settings. Transparency reduces cancellation anxiety.

Priority 4: Report Onboarding

First-time tooltip for mastery tiers. Helps new parents understand what "Mastery" means in context without requiring them to figure it out.

Biggest Risk

Untested parent comprehension at scale. Phase 2 priority: comprehension test with 5 parents -- show a report screen and ask them to identify strongest topic, weakest topic, and trend direction.

Despite the open questions, the validation data told a clear story.
08 -- Resolution

Under 50 seconds. Every signal understood.

The client correctly identified her daughter's strongest skill area, weakest skill area, and week-over-week trend direction from the report screen alone -- all under 40 seconds. This confirmed the 4-tier mastery visualization is legible to non-expert parents.
