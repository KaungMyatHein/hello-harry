# Orchestra -- Presentation Script

**Total duration:** 6-7 minutes
**Format:** Portfolio walkthrough / design talk
**Tone:** Conversational, reflective, confident

---

## Opening -- Set the Stage

**Time: ~45 seconds**

*[Stand relaxed. No slides yet -- or show the Orchestra site hero. Make eye contact.]*

> So most of the work I do has a brief. There's a business problem, stakeholders, constraints, timelines. And I love that kind of work -- it's where real design happens.
>
> But every now and then, I need to step away from all of that and ask myself a simpler question: what do I actually believe about visual design?
>
> Orchestra was my answer to that question. It's a side project -- no client, no deliverable, no deadline. Just me exploring composition for its own sake.

*[Pause briefly. Let the idea land.]*

---

## 01 -- Concept: Design Without a Client

**Time: ~1 minute 15 seconds**

*[Navigate to or show Section 1 of the case study. Point to the quote card if presenting on screen.]*

> The name "Orchestra" came from this idea that a page is a composition. Type, space, weight -- every element is performing in relation to every other element. Change one thing and everything shifts.
>
> I wanted to build something where the only success metric was: does this feel right? Not "does it convert," not "does it match the brand guidelines." Just -- does it feel considered?
>
> There's a quote I kept coming back to during this project: "Good designers know the rules. Great designers know when the rules are the constraint." And that became the philosophy for the whole thing. I wasn't trying to break rules. I was trying to figure out which ones were actually helping me and which ones I was just following out of habit.

*[Transition naturally.]*

> So let me walk you through what I actually explored.

---

## 02 -- Composition: Rhythm, Weight, and Whitespace

**Time: ~1 minute 30 seconds**

*[Show Section 2. Reference the three composition cards visually.]*

> The project focused on three compositional principles. Not as theory -- I've read about these a thousand times. I wanted to internalize them as felt practice. I worked through dozens of variations before each one clicked.
>
> The first is **rhythm**. Consistent vertical cadence. You can create readability without explicit grids if your spacing has a reliable pulse. I spent a lot of time just adjusting line heights and section gaps until the page had a natural breathing pattern.
>
> The second is **weight**. Contrast in size and tone. You can guide someone's eye across a page without arrows, without labels, without any explicit wayfinding -- just by making intentional choices about what's heavy and what's light.
>
> And the third is **whitespace**. This is the one designers talk about the most but practice the least. Empty space is not the absence of design. It is design. The best compositions I made during this project were the ones where I kept removing elements until only the essential ones remained.

*[Brief pause.]*

> These three principles sound simple. They are simple. But simple is not easy. It took iteration after iteration to get each layout to feel balanced.

---

## 03 -- Publishing: The Design IS the Code

**Time: ~1 minute 15 seconds**

*[Show Section 3. Reference the "What Worked" and "The Limits" cards.]*

> Here's where it gets interesting from a tooling perspective. Orchestra was built and published entirely inside Figma Sites. No export. No handoff. No developer. What I designed on the canvas is exactly what shipped to the live URL.
>
> And that was a deliberate choice. I wanted zero translation loss. In most projects, there's always some gap between the design file and what actually gets built. Figma Sites eliminated that gap completely.
>
> What worked really well: direct publishing from the canvas, instant iteration -- I could change something and publish it in seconds -- and the output was pixel-perfect to what I'd designed.
>
> But there were real limits. No custom interaction logic, so I couldn't build anything beyond basic scroll and hover. Font choices were constrained to what Figma Sites supports. And I couldn't embed video or rich media.

*[Gesture or nod to acknowledge the trade-off.]*

> For this project, those limits were fine -- honestly, they were features. Constraints kept me focused on the composition itself rather than getting distracted by animation or interactivity.

---

## 04 -- Reflection: What It Gave Me Back

**Time: ~1 minute 30 seconds**

*[Show Section 4. Slow down the pace here -- this is the emotional core.]*

> So what did I actually take away from this?
>
> Side projects without clients teach you what you actually believe about design. Not what you've been told to believe by a brand guide or a stakeholder review. Your real defaults. Your actual taste.
>
> And I learned two things about myself.
>
> **First: constraints reveal taste.** Without a brief, your aesthetic defaults are completely exposed. There's nowhere to hide. I discovered that I lean toward severe typographic hierarchy and generous negative space. That's useful to know about yourself as a designer. It tells you what your instincts are when nobody else is in the room.
>
> **Second: process without pressure is underrated.** The absence of a deadline produced more considered decisions. Not every project should move fast. Some things only become clear when you're not rushing toward a handoff. Orchestra reminded me that restraint is a skill -- and the best compositions I made were the ones with the fewest elements.

*[Pause. Let the reflection settle.]*

---

## Closing -- Bring It Home

**Time: ~30 seconds**

*[Make eye contact. Speak with conviction.]*

> Orchestra is small. It's a single-page site. But it changed how I approach every layout I touch in my day-to-day work. It gave me a sharper eye for when something is working and when it's just busy.
>
> If you're curious, the site is live at orchestra.figma.site. And I'd love to hear what you think.

*[Smile. Open for questions.]*

## Presentation Tips

- **Pace:** Speak slower during the Reflection section. It carries the emotional weight.
- **Pauses:** Use silence after the opening question and after each composition principle. Let the audience absorb.
- **Visuals:** If presenting alongside the live case study, scroll to each section as you reach it. The snap-scroll transitions create natural beats.
- **Hands:** When describing rhythm, weight, and whitespace, use your hands to gesture spacing and hierarchy. Physical movement reinforces abstract concepts.
- **Eye contact:** Look up from notes during the quote and during both lesson statements. These are your strongest lines.
- **If time is short:** Cut the tooling section (03 -- Publishing) to 45 seconds by skipping the "What Worked / Limits" detail and just stating the Figma Sites approach in two sentences. This brings total to ~5:45.
- **If time is long:** Expand the Composition section by describing a specific iteration that failed before you landed on the final layout. Personal anecdotes add 30-60 seconds naturally.
- **Q&A prep:** Expect questions about why Figma Sites over a coded portfolio, and about how this side project influenced client work. Have one concrete example ready for each.
