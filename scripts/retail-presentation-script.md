# Retail POS Redesign -- Presentation Script

**Project:** From Feedback to 427% ROI
**Company:** Raytail
**Role:** UX Designer
**Total Duration:** 6-7 minutes

---

## 01 -- The Problem: The Red Flag
**Time: ~1 min 15 sec**

> I want to start with a single sentence. A sentence I overheard at a workshop in the Maldives.
>
> *(pause, let the quote land)*
>
> "Cashiers are struggling with the new return process. They can't understand the design... but we don't know why."
>
> That was it. That was all I needed to hear.
>
> So I didn't open a dashboard. I didn't pull up analytics. I went to the shop floor and I watched cashiers work. Real transactions, real pressure, real queues.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> And what I saw was painful. Every hidden button, every missing label cost seconds. Not dramatic seconds -- the kind nobody notices individually. But multiply a 30-second delay by thousands of transactions per day, across every store in the chain -- and you are bleeding money.
>
> The POS wasn't broken. It was invisibly expensive.
>
> I needed to find exactly where the friction lived -- and prove it with data.
>
> *(scroll to next section)*

---

## 02 -- Audit: Four Features, All Invisible
**Time: ~1 min**

> So I audited the interface against the tasks cashiers actually performed most frequently. Four features stood out. Not because they were complex -- because they were hidden.
>
> *(gesture to the old UI screenshot)*
>
> Returns -- buried so deep that cashiers couldn't find the button without calling a manager. Every single time.
>
> Day End -- icon only, no text label. New hires had zero chance.
>
> Discounts -- two clicks too many for something done dozens of times per shift.
>
> And Reprint -- high frequency, completely invisible.
>
> Four critical daily tasks. All invisible in the interface.
>
> But here's the question that mattered: which one do we fix first? And how do we decide without guessing?
>
> *(scroll to next section)*

---

## 03 -- Research: The Two-Lens Method
**Time: ~1 min 30 sec**

> Most teams would ask stakeholders what matters most and start designing. I had a hunch that business priorities and user pain wouldn't line up -- and the gap between them is where the real opportunity would be.
>
> So I ran the same importance-ranking exercise twice.
>
> First lens -- Business. Internal stakeholders voted on each activity, one-to-five scale. I built a heat map of what leadership valued.
>
> Second lens -- Users. I asked the exact same questions to real cashiers on the shop floor. Same format, same scale. A second heat map -- this one from the people who actually use the system eight hours a day.
>
> Then I overlaid both maps.
>
> *(pause -- this is the turning point)*
>
> And the surprise? Returns. Business rated them low -- "returns are rare." But cashiers rated them as their number one frustration.
>
> Why? Returns weren't frequent. But every single return had a customer standing right there, watching, waiting. The per-incident impact was enormous -- and completely invisible in the aggregate data.
>
> *(gesture to data)*
>
> Now we had evidence-based priorities. Not opinions -- evidence.

---

## Persona: The Cashier
**Time: ~30 sec**

> Let me ground this in the person we were designing for. The cashier. Front-line retail operator. Hundreds of transactions per shift. Constant time pressure -- a queue of customers always watching. And here's the thing: nobody formally trained them. They learned the system through trial and error.
>
> Their clarity score for Day End was 0.50. For Returns -- 0.85 after redesign, but the old flow was the source of their biggest frustration.
>
> *(scroll to next section)*

---

## 04 -- Design: Prototype and Test
**Time: ~45 sec**

> I restructured the menu around two principles: group by relationship, sort by frequency. The mid-fidelity prototype exposed every critical action on the primary screen -- no more hunting through menus.
>
> *(gesture to the prototype screenshot)*
>
> Then I tested it. Not with hypothetical tasks -- with the actual workflows cashiers ran daily, using real transaction scenarios.
>
> *(gesture to clarity scores)*
>
> Return scored 0.85 -- the highest clarity score. The exact feature that started this project. The one business had dismissed as "rare." The data validated the two-lens approach: we were fixing the right thing.
>
> But clarity scores only tell you if people can find things. The real question was whether it would actually save time at scale.

---

## 05 -- Solution: What Changed
**Time: ~45 sec**

> Four structural changes. Each one solving a specific pain point from the audit.
>
> *(gesture to final design)*
>
> Grouped activities -- related actions in one place, no more hunting across menus. Sorted by frequency -- the interface now reflects how cashiers actually work, not how engineers organized the database. Icon plus text labels on 48-pixel touch targets -- new hires no longer have to memorize cryptic icons. And product-level actions -- one tap on the product, not a detour through a separate menu.
>
> The prototype tested well. But could we put a number on the business impact?
>
> *(scroll to next section)*

---

## 06 -- Results: The Numbers
**Time: ~45 sec**

> Every second saved compounds. Here is what the redesign delivered.
>
> *(pause, let the metrics land one at a time)*
>
> 427 percent ROI. Measured, not estimated. The design cost paid for itself within six months.
>
> *(gesture to data)*
>
> Over 50 percent time reduction on returns -- from 60 seconds down to under 30. Across every cashier, every store.
>
> Faster onboarding. Icon-plus-text labeling cut dependency on memorization entirely.
>
> *(gesture to before-and-after table)*
>
> Returns: one minute down to under 30 seconds. Day End: same. Receipt Reprint: same. Transactions became consistent under 10 seconds.
>
> The redesign didn't just make things faster. It made the cost of UX debt visible -- and gave stakeholders a framework to evaluate future design investments.

---

## 07 -- Reflection: What This Project Taught Me
**Time: ~1 min**

> Four lessons from this project that I carry with me.
>
> First -- we almost redesigned the wrong thing. The initial stakeholder conversation pointed us toward the sale flow. It was only when I watched cashiers directly -- not through reports, not through surveys -- that returns emerged as the real friction point. Observation beats assumption every time.
>
> Second -- low frequency does not mean low impact. Returns were "rare" in aggregate. But every slow return had a customer standing at the counter, watching, waiting. Impact per incident was enormous.
>
> Third -- design debt compounds like financial debt. The assumption that "returns are rare" wasn't wrong once -- it was wrong for years. Hidden costs that grew with every new store, every new cashier, every deployment.
>
> And fourth -- data wins stakeholder buy-in. The two-lens heat map made prioritization objective. The conversation shifted from "I think we should" to "the data shows we should."
>
> *(pause)*
>
> If I could go back, I'd do one thing differently: run testing in actual stores during peak hours, not in controlled lab conditions. Cashiers in a quiet room behave differently than cashiers during rush hour. That's an edge case only real-world observation can surface.
>
> Thank you.

## Presentation Tips

1. **Open with silence.** Before you say the quote, pause for two full seconds. Let the audience settle.

2. **The quote is your hook.** Deliver it slowly, as if you're remembering the moment. Don't rush past it -- it sets the emotional frame for the entire talk.

3. **Use the "invisible expense" framing early.** The phrase "invisibly expensive" is your thesis. Return to it when you reveal the numbers at the end -- it creates a satisfying arc.

4. **Let the two-lens surprise breathe.** When you reveal that business rated returns low but cashiers rated them number one -- pause. That gap is the most compelling moment in the story. Don't explain it immediately. Let the audience feel the disconnect first.

5. **Numbers land better in isolation.** When you say "427 percent ROI," stop. Don't immediately follow with the next metric. Give each number its own beat.

6. **Gesture to visuals, don't read them.** When the UI screenshots or data cards are on screen, point and reference them. Never read bullet points verbatim from the screen.

7. **End on the reflection, not the metrics.** The lessons are what make this memorable. Anyone can show a before-and-after. The self-awareness about lab testing vs. real-world observation shows maturity as a designer.

8. **Practice the transitions.** The weakest moments in any case study presentation are the transitions between sections. Each section ends with a hook question -- use those to build momentum into the next section.

9. **Keep your hands visible.** When presenting design work, open gestures toward the screen signal confidence and invite the audience into the work.

10. **If time is tight, cut the Persona section.** It reinforces the problem but doesn't advance the narrative. The core arc works without it: Problem, Audit, Research, Design, Results, Reflection.
