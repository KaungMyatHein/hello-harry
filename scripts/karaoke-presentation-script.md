# Presentation Script: COVID Killed Karaoke Night

**Case Study:** Designing a Next-Gen Karaoke App for Myanmar
**Presenter:** Harry Hein, Product Design Owner
**Target Duration:** 6-7 minutes
**Tone:** Conversational professional, story-driven

---

## Opening Hook (0:00 - 0:30)

*[Stand center. No slides yet -- or show the hero image of the app UI. Pause before speaking.]*

> Imagine it's 2021. You're in Yangon. Every karaoke bar in the country just went dark. Not because the music stopped -- because COVID shut the doors.

> Karaoke in Myanmar isn't a novelty. It's how people decompress, connect, and feel something together. And overnight, that was gone.

> One research participant told us: "I miss singing with friends... lockdown is so boring. I need social entertainment at home."

> So we asked ourselves -- can we build the stage back inside their phones?

---

## 01 -- The Problem (0:30 - 1:15)

*[Advance to problem framing. Slow your pace here -- let the gap land.]*

> Digital alternatives existed. Smule, Joox, Star Maker -- they were out there. But none of them were built for this market.

> They lacked Myanmar songs. They couldn't accept local payments. And they felt nothing like the bar experience people actually craved.

> Myanmar had no localized karaoke app. And the gap wasn't just a product gap -- it was a cultural one.

> But here's the question that shaped the entire project: were people missing the mic... or the moment?

---

## 02 -- Research (1:15 - 2:15)

*[Advance to research findings. Use your hands to count off the three themes.]*

> We interviewed 20 karaoke enthusiasts, casual singers, and music lovers across four research phases. We weren't fishing for feature requests -- we were looking for emotional patterns.

> Three themes jumped out immediately.

> First -- it's social, not solo. Users went to bars for the atmosphere, not just the mic. Even people with home karaoke machines still went out. So whatever we built had to feel communal.

> Second -- singing is stress relief. 80 percent of participants described karaoke as therapeutic. This wasn't entertainment. It was emotional regulation. That meant the UX had to protect that feeling, not interrupt it.

> Third -- subscriptions feel natural. 80 percent already used Netflix. We didn't need to educate anyone on the payment model. We just needed to demonstrate value fast.

> So the real design challenge became: how do we design for emotion, not just function?

---

## 03 -- Key Insights (2:15 - 3:00)

*[Advance to the three metrics. Point to each number as you say it.]*

> Amid all that qualitative richness, three data points crystallized our direction. Each one eliminated a debate and replaced it with a decision.

> 65 percent search by artist first. So we made artist search the default entry point. Friction dropped immediately.

> 70 percent have a favorite genre. That's a clear signal for personalization -- the home screen could surface relevant content from day one.

> And 85 percent use KBZ Pay -- Myanmar's dominant mobile payment method. Skip it and you lose the market. We made it the primary checkout option.

> We had the data. Now we needed to decide what to build -- and what to cut.

---

## 04 -- Competitive Analysis (3:00 - 3:30)

*[Brief section. Keep it crisp -- three competitors, three lessons.]*

> We studied three established platforms. Not to copy -- to extract principles.

> From Smule: community is the product, not a feature bolted on later. From Joox: without catalogue depth, nothing else matters. From Star Maker: emotion beats perfection. Users want to feel like a star, not be judged like one.

> Every competitor validated the same insight from our research. But none of them served Myanmar's language, payment systems, or music catalogue. That was our opening.

---

## 05 -- MVP Scoping (3:30 - 4:15)

*[Advance to the Keep/Cut/Defer table. This is a tension moment -- slow down.]*

> With limited dev resources and a market window closing fast, every feature had to earn its place. We split the backlog into three buckets: keep, cut, and defer.

> We kept basic karaoke with artist search, subscription with KBZ Pay, and song feedback.

> We cut social rooms, video recording, and advanced recommendations.

> And we deferred multiplayer, community challenges, AR effects, and live streaming to future releases.

> Social rooms were the hardest cut. Our research showed community was central to the karaoke experience. But the engineering cost was three times the entire MVP budget. So we deferred it -- and designed the architecture so it could be added later without a rewrite.

*[Pause. Let that trade-off land.]*

---

## 06 -- Information Architecture (4:15 - 4:45)

*[Show user flow diagram if available.]*

> The user flow had to feel effortless. From opening the app to singing the first note, we mapped every decision point and every potential drop-off.

> The critical insight here: onboarding had to end with a song, not a settings screen. Users needed to feel the product's value within 60 seconds of opening it.

---

## 07 -- Wireframes (4:45 - 5:05)

*[Show wireframe image. Keep this brief -- it's a bridge section.]*

> We ran through three rounds of low-fidelity wireframes with the team. Artist search lived front and center. Genre filters sat one tap away. And the singing screen stripped away everything except lyrics and controls -- protecting the emotional flow we identified in research.

---

## 08 -- Visual Design (5:05 - 5:35)

*[Show the final UI. Let the visual do some of the talking.]*

> The visual direction drew straight from karaoke bar aesthetics. Electric accents, dark backgrounds, playful typography, high contrast. The goal was simple: make the phone screen feel like a stage.

> Every color choice, every animation timing, every transition was tested against one question: does this make the user feel like they're performing -- or just using an app?

---

## 09 -- Reflection (5:35 - 6:15)

*[No slides needed. Speak directly to the audience.]*

> Three lessons from this project.

> One -- defaults compound. Making artist search the default was one decision. But it cascaded through everything: search results, recommendations, onboarding. One good default freed up cognitive load across millions of taps.

> Two -- local payment equals local product. KBZ Pay wasn't a nice-to-have. It was the conversion gateway. In emerging markets, payment choice is make-or-break.

> Three -- constraints breed clarity. The tight timeline forced MVP rigor. The features we cut taught us as much as the ones we built. Social rooms were the right feature -- just not the right feature for v1.

---

## Close -- Time Machine (6:15 - 6:45)

*[This is your vulnerability moment. Be honest and reflective.]*

> If I could go back, here's what I'd change. I assumed social features belonged in the MVP. What I missed was that users wanted to practice alone first, before any social exposure.

> What I'd do differently: add a lightweight social layer -- progress sharing, friend activity feeds -- before committing to full social rooms. I think that would have doubled engagement without the pressure of live group performance.

> Thank you. I'd love to take any questions.

*[Pause. Smile. Wait for questions.]*

## Delivery Tips

1. **Pause after the opening quote.** Let the audience sit with the human moment before you transition to the problem frame.
2. **Count on your fingers** when listing the three research themes and the three reflections. It gives the audience a visual anchor.
3. **Slow down for the MVP cut.** Social rooms being cut is your strongest tension beat. Rushing past it loses the drama.
4. **Let the final UI breathe.** When you show the visual design, stop talking for two to three seconds and let the audience look.
5. **Own the "Time Machine" section.** Vulnerability builds credibility. Don't rush through what you got wrong.
6. **If running short on time,** compress Sections 06 and 07 (IA and Wireframes) into a single 20-second bridge.
7. **If running long,** cut the Competitive Analysis section to a single sentence and move the key takeaway into the Insights section.
8. **Audience Q&A prep:** Be ready to answer why you cut social rooms, how you validated KBZ Pay as the primary payment rail, and what happened after launch.
