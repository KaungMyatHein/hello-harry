# Presentation Script: From Daily Ritual to 104% ROI

**Case Study:** TreeDots Operations Dashboard Redesign
**Presenter:** Harry Hein
**Total Duration:** 6-7 minutes
**Tone:** Conversational, story-driven, confident

---

## Opening — The Hook (0:30)

*[Stand still, make eye contact, pause before speaking]*

> I want to tell you about Mei.

> Every single morning, Mei — the ops manager at TreeDots — sat down and did the same thing. She opened Xero for revenue numbers. She copied them into a spreadsheet. She checked the supplier portal for payments. Twelve copy-pastes. Thirty minutes. Every day, before her real work even started.

> And here's the thing nobody questioned: by the time she was done, the data was already stale. The whole team was making decisions on yesterday's numbers.

*[Pause. Let that land.]*

> What if we could give her those 30 minutes back?

---

## 01 — The Problem: The Morning Ritual (0:45)

*[Advance to problem section]*

> So let me break down what was actually happening. Three separate tools. Xero for revenue and expenses — its own login. Spreadsheets for manual compilation — twelve copy-pastes every morning, and one misplaced decimal could cascade through the whole report. Then the supplier portal with yet another login, where data arrived on its own schedule.

> The pain wasn't that the data was missing. Every number existed somewhere. It just never existed in one place at the same time.

---

## 02 — Research: Two Perspectives, One Pain (1:00)

*[Shift posture — this is the empathy section]*

> I shadowed Mei's workflow and interviewed five stakeholders across finance and operations. Two quotes told me everything I needed to know.

> Mei said: "I know exactly what numbers the team needs. I just don't have them in one place."

> And the Finance Lead said: "We make decisions based on Mei's reports. But sometimes the numbers are a day behind."

*[Gesture between two imaginary people]*

> Same problem, different angle. Mei spent her time assembling data. Finance received stale data. Both lost. The gap wasn't missing information — it was scattered information.

> The data existed. The design didn't.

---

## 02b — Persona: Mei (0:30)

*[Keep this brief — reinforce, don't repeat]*

> So our primary user is Mei, the ops manager. Thirty-minute morning ritual, twelve copy-pastes, no validation, no sync. Amendments buried three clicks deep in the nav. And a finance team making decisions on day-old numbers because real-time data simply didn't exist yet.

---

## 03 — Audit: Pain Points in the Old Design (0:45)

*[Point to the old UI if showing slides]*

> When I audited the existing interface, two structural problems jumped out.

> First, the amendments page was buried. Finance menu, then Amendments submenu — three clicks to reach a task Mei did dozens of times a day.

> Second, there was no status clarity. Manual acknowledgement, no Xero sync, no visual hierarchy. Mei couldn't tell at a glance which amendments needed action and which were already handled.

> We knew what was broken. The question became: what should the fix actually look like?

---

## Success Metrics (0:20)

*[Quick and confident — set expectations before the reveal]*

> Before designing anything, we defined success. Design KPIs: zero minutes of morning report prep, and real-time data freshness. Business KPIs: projected ROI above 100%, and zero cross-tool copy-pastes. Those were our targets.

---

## 04 — Design: What Changed (1:00)

*[This is the reveal — show energy here]*

> Four changes, each mapped directly to a pain point from the audit.

> First, an alert banner. Actionable notification for pending amendments — no more hunting through menus.

> Second, Xero sync status. Color-coded indicators at a glance. Green means synced, red means action needed. No ambiguity.

> Third, amendment badges. "Need Acknowledgement" visible right on each row. The interface now tells Mei what to do, not just what exists.

> Fourth, color-coded rows for payment status. Scan, don't search.

*[Advance to amendment details]*

> And for the amendment detail view — three improvements. Side-by-side comparison of original versus amended, so no more flipping between tabs. Item-level price diffs and savings. And one-click acknowledge — a task that used to take five clicks now takes one.

> The amendment flow was fixed. But could we solve the bigger problem — the morning ritual itself?

---

## 05 — Solution: One Screen, Every KPI (0:30)

*[Slow down here — this is the thesis]*

> The real solution wasn't fixing individual pages. It was eliminating the need to visit multiple tools entirely.

> Revenue auto-refreshed from Xero. Orders updated in real-time. Fulfillment rate calculated live. Pending payments surfaced with action buttons. No tab-switching. No copy-pasting. One screen.

---

## 06 — Results: Before and After (0:45)

*[Deliver metrics with confidence — pause between each]*

> Let me walk through the before and after.

> Data sources: three tools down to one dashboard. Daily prep time: thirty minutes down to zero. Data freshness: day-old to real-time. Manual steps: twelve copy-pastes down to zero. Error risk: high to near-zero.

*[Pause]*

> 104% ROI. Here's the math: thirty minutes a day, times 250 working days, equals 125 hours recovered per year. At $11 an hour, that's roughly $1,375 saved — against a design cost of about $1,360. Break-even at month six.

> And here's something I didn't expect: the KPI card component was adopted downstream and became part of TreeDots' internal design system, extending the impact well beyond this single project.

---

## 07 — Validation: The Quote (0:30)

*[This is the emotional peak — deliver it simply]*

> On the first morning after launch, Mei messaged me and said:

*[Pause]*

> "What do I do with my morning now?"

*[Let it sit for a beat]*

> That was the best user feedback I've ever received. She didn't miss the thirty minutes. She'd already forgotten what she used to do with them. The design didn't just save time — it made an entire category of daily friction disappear so completely that the person who suffered from it most couldn't remember it existed.

---

## 08 — Reflection: Four Lessons (0:45)

*[Measured, thoughtful pace]*

> Four things I took away from this project.

> One — time savings compound at scale. Half an hour a day sounds small. Across a year, it's 125 hours. Three extra work weeks.

> Two — the data existed, the design didn't. No new APIs were needed. The entire ROI came from restructuring information architecture. The most valuable design work doesn't add — it reorganizes.

> Three — reducing tools beats adding features. Three systems to one dashboard. The highest-impact decision was subtraction.

> Four — remote research works with the right questions. Interviewing Mei about her daily routine surfaced both the pain points and the time-cost data we needed for ROI. You don't need a lab. You need the right questions and the willingness to watch someone work.

---

## Time Machine / Close (0:20)

*[Honest, self-aware]*

> If I could go back, I'd run an A/B test with time-tracking — old workflow versus new, with stopwatch data. We had strong anecdotal evidence, but hard numbers would have made the business case bulletproof from day one.

> Thank you.

## Presentation Tips

- **Mei is the anchor.** Every section connects back to her story. If you lose the thread, return to Mei.
- **Pause after the key quote.** "What do I do with my morning now?" needs silence around it. Don't rush past the strongest moment.
- **Metrics land better isolated.** Say the number, pause, then explain. Don't bury 104% ROI in the middle of a sentence.
- **The math is your credibility.** 30 min x 250 days = 125 hours. Rehearse this so it sounds natural, not rehearsed.
- **Subtraction is the thesis.** Three tools to one. Twelve steps to zero. Every design decision was about removing, not adding. Make that philosophy explicit.
- **End on the human, not the number.** The ROI proves value. Mei's quote proves impact. Close with the quote, not the spreadsheet.
- **If you only have 5 minutes:** Cut 02b (Persona), Success Metrics, and Time Machine. The story holds without them.
